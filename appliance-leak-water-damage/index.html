Appliance Leak Water Damage in Santa Monica: What to Know

Three appliances cause most of the leaks we see. When the supply hose on a washing machine splits, water flows under full pressure until somebody turns it off. A dishwasher tends to leak slowly from a door seal or drain hose, out of sight under the cabinets. Behind a refrigerator, the thin tube feeding the ice maker may drip for months. Each one needs a different kind of cleanup.

Our Santa Monica crew starts by pulling the appliance out and finding where the water went. For a kitchen, the usual places are below the flooring and under the cabinets. In laundry rooms it means the wall behind the machine and the rooms next door, or the ceiling below if the laundry is upstairs. Then our team removes the standing water, meters the materials that soaked it up, and places equipment matched to the size of the damage.

Do This First

Before We Arrive

  1. Shut the Machine DownCancel the cycle, and pull the plug only when the floor and the outlet near it are dry. Otherwise, use the breaker to cut its power.
  2. Shut Off the Water LineWashers have hot and cold valves on the wall behind them. The dishwasher valve is under the kitchen sink. The small valve for an ice maker line sits behind the refrigerator or underneath.
  3. Soak Up Standing WaterGetting reachable water up with a mop or towels stops it from moving under the cabinets and the floor. Use a vacuum only if it is a wet and dry model.
  4. Keep the Door ClosedA dishwasher or washer that still holds water should stay closed. When the door opens mid-cycle, the full load of water can pour out on the floor.
  5. Document What FailedKeep the part that failed, whether a hose, a fitting or a valve, and get photos of it. It may be requested by the manufacturer of the appliance and by your insurance company.
Why It Happens

Why This Happens

  • A dishwasher that was installed out of level or with a supply connection left loose.
  • Washer supply hoses made of rubber that get weak over the years and burst from the pressure.
  • A kinked or cracked ice maker line, or one whose fitting has worked loose.
  • The water inlet valve on a washer or dishwasher failing to close, which lets the machine overfill.
  • Too much detergent or an overloaded drum that makes the machine overflow.
Why Speed Matters

What the Water Damages

Baseboards and Wall Bottoms

On a flooded kitchen or laundry floor, the baseboards and the lower edge of the drywall draw water upward. Since it is out of view, the wall behind the machine tends to be wetter than the rest.

Mold Behind Appliances

Hardly anyone looks at the dark, warm space behind a dishwasher or refrigerator. Mold on the floor and wall can be fed by a slow drip in that spot well before it is noticed.

Kitchen Cabinets

Dishwasher leaks soak cabinet sides and toe kicks from underneath. Panels swell and come apart, and a wet cabinet box can keep the wall behind it damp for weeks.

Rooms Next Door

Water may pour from a washer hose for hours on end. It passes under walls into hallways, closets and bedrooms, and soaks carpet and pad well away from the laundry room.

Will Insurance Pay?

What About Insurance?

When a machine overflows unexpectedly or a hose bursts, that is typically treated as sudden and accidental, which many homeowners policies cover. Payment typically goes toward the damage the water did, and the appliance or the part that failed is not included. A claim is harder when an ice maker line or dishwasher seeped slowly, since coverage usually does not extend to gradual leaks. Certain insurers will want to know the hose's age and whether it was kept up. Hold on to the part that failed, photograph everything ahead of cleanup, and review what your policy says.

What It Costs

What Affects the Cost

  • Any mold growth, because it must be dealt with ahead of drying.
  • Whether cabinets and flooring can be dried in place or need to be removed.
  • Whether this was a sudden flood or slow seepage, and which machine it came from.
  • The time that passed before anyone found the leak, which matters most for slow drips at the back of a unit.

What Happens After You Call

Move the Appliance and Inspect

We disconnect and move the appliance, confirm the supply is off, and trace the water path under cabinets, beneath flooring and into nearby walls with moisture meters and thermal imaging.

2

Remove Standing Water

Standing water is extracted from floors and carpet. To reach water caught below cabinets or under floating floors, our crew pulls toe kicks or takes up boards.

3

We set air movers and dehumidifiers and, where possible, dry cabinets and hardwood in place with directed airflow so they do not have to be torn out.

4

Clean and Confirm

Any surface that dishwasher or washer discharge reached is treated with an antimicrobial. Before the appliance is put back, a last set of readings confirms the floor under it is no longer damp.
